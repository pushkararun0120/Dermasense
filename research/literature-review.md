# Dermasense – Literature Review

## 1. Introduction

Wound care is an important part of healthcare, and proper wound monitoring helps healthcare professionals assess changes during the healing process. Conventional wound dressings primarily provide wound coverage, manage moisture, and protect the wound environment. However, they do not always provide a direct visual indication of changes in the wound environment.

Recent research has explored hydrogel-based dressings and smart wound dressings that can respond to changes in wound-related parameters, including pH, temperature, moisture, and other biochemical indicators.

Dermasense is a proposed wound-patch concept that combines a hydrogel dressing with a pH-responsive colour indicator. The idea is to make changes in wound-fluid pH visible without requiring electronic sensors or batteries.

This literature review examines published research on wound pH, hydrogel wound dressings, colour-changing sensing systems, and the limitations that must be considered when developing such a product.

## 2. Wound pH and Its Relationship with Healing

The pH of the wound environment is one of several parameters researchers have investigated for understanding wound healing. Changes in pH may be associated with changes in the wound environment, including processes related to tissue repair and microbial activity.

Research has often observed that chronic or non-healing wounds can have a more alkaline environment, while a shift towards a more acidic environment may be associated with improvements in some healing wounds. However, wound pH is influenced by multiple factors, and these patterns are not universal.

Power, Moore, and O'Connor (2017) conducted a systematic review examining pH, wound-exudate composition, and temperature as potential indicators of wound-healing outcomes. Their review found promising associations but concluded that the available evidence was insufficient to confidently recommend these measurements as reliable predictors of healing. The researchers also noted differences in study methods and limitations in the quality of the evidence.

Tarricone et al. (2020) reviewed the relationship between wound pH and healing rates in chronic wounds. Their review further highlighted the need for better evidence and more structured approaches to understanding the relationship between pH and wound recovery.

These studies suggest that wound pH may provide useful supplementary information, but it should not be interpreted independently of other clinical findings.

For Dermasense, this means that the proposed colour indicator should communicate a change in pH rather than directly label a wound as infected or healthy.

## 3. Hydrogel-Based Wound Dressings

Hydrogels are three-dimensional polymer networks capable of absorbing and retaining water. Their properties depend on the polymers used, the crosslinking method, and the final formulation.

Su et al. (2021) reviewed hydrogel preparation methods and biomaterials used in wound dressings. The review discussed the potential of hydrogels to maintain a moist environment and support wound-dressing functions. It also covered natural and synthetic materials, preparation methods, and challenges associated with developing hydrogel dressings.

Hydrogel properties can vary considerably. Water absorption, mechanical strength, permeability, stability, and compatibility with other components depend on the formulation. Therefore, not every hydrogel is automatically suitable for wound contact or for use as a sensing material.

Hydrogels are relevant to Dermasense because the product concept requires a fluid-contact layer capable of holding a pH-responsive indicator.

However, the selected material would need to satisfy two sets of requirements:

- It must provide appropriate physical and fluid-handling properties for the intended dressing design.
- It must allow the incorporated indicator to respond to pH changes in a consistent and visible way.

The relationship between these requirements makes material selection and testing important stages of the development process.

## 4. Colourimetric pH-Sensing Wound Dressings

Colourimetric sensing uses a visible colour change to communicate a change in a measured parameter. In pH-responsive systems, the colour-producing material responds to differences in the acidity or alkalinity of the surrounding environment.

Compared with electronic sensing systems, a colourimetric approach can potentially provide a simple visual response without requiring a battery or electronic display. However, its usefulness depends on the indicator, sensing material, viewing conditions, and ability to interpret the resulting colour.

Pang et al. (2023) reviewed developments in smart wound dressings for advanced wound management. Their review discussed the monitoring of wound-related biomarkers using approaches including colourimetric, fluorimetric, and electrochemical sensing. It also highlighted the development of dressings that combine monitoring with other functions.

This research demonstrates that wound monitoring is an active area of materials and biomedical research. It also indicates that visual sensing is one approach within a broader range of developing technologies.

For Dermasense, colourimetric sensing is relevant because the proposed design prioritises direct visual observation rather than electronic monitoring.

Nevertheless, a visible response alone does not establish that a sensing system is accurate, clinically meaningful, or suitable for use on a wound. These properties require separate evaluation.

## 5. Research on pH-Responsive Hydrogel Patches

### 5.1 Liu et al. (2017): A pH-Indicating Colorimetric Tough Hydrogel Patch

Liu et al. (2017) developed a pH-indicating hydrogel patch intended for applications in smart wound dressings. The researchers incorporated a modified form of phenol red into an alginate/polyacrylamide hydrogel system.

An important aspect of their approach was modifying the indicator so that it could be incorporated into the polymer network. This was intended to reduce dye leaching, a potential problem when an indicator is simply mixed into a material.

The researchers evaluated colour response and several physical properties of the hydrogel, including its mechanical behaviour, swelling, and water-vapour transmission.

Their results demonstrated that a hydrogel patch could produce visible colour changes at different pH values while retaining useful physical characteristics under the tested conditions.

The study is particularly relevant to Dermasense because it demonstrates a possible approach to combining hydrogel materials with a pH-responsive visual indicator.

However, the study evaluated a specific engineered formulation. Its results cannot automatically be applied to a different hydrogel, indicator concentration, or patch design. It also does not establish that Dermasense would be clinically effective or safe.

Reference: Liu et al. (2017), *Polymers*, 9(11), 558.

### 5.2 Hoveizavi et al. (2025): Colourimetric pH-Responsive Nanofibrous Hydrogels

Hoveizavi et al. (2025) investigated colourimetric pH-responsive nanofibrous hydrogels for in-vitro wound-monitoring applications. Their research used bromothymol blue (BTB) as a pH-sensitive indicator within a specialised PVA/Gel-A nanofibrous material.

The study investigated the colour response at different pH values and examined whether the indicator could be retained in the material. The researchers found that adding a cationic complexing agent reduced dye release compared with the formulation without that agent. They also conducted an in-vitro cytotoxicity assessment using keratinocytes.

The study demonstrates the importance of investigating both sensing performance and indicator retention. A clear colour response is not sufficient if the indicator is released from the material or if other safety requirements are not met.

The reported results apply to the particular formulation and test conditions used in the study. The specialised Gel-A material should not be assumed to be interchangeable with ordinary gelatin, and the study's findings do not establish the clinical safety of other hydrogel formulations.

This research is relevant to Dermasense because BTB is one of the indicators considered for further investigation. It also highlights indicator immobilisation and release testing as important development considerations.

Reference: Hoveizavi et al. (2025), *Journal of Materials Chemistry B*, 13, 11621–11639.

## 6. Reviews of Optical pH-Sensing Technologies

Recent research has examined a range of optical approaches for monitoring wound pH.

A 2025 review by the authors of *Optically pH-Sensing in Smart Wound Dressings towards Real-Time Monitoring of Wound States* examined colourimetric dyes, fluorescent sensing materials, and different approaches to communicating colour-based measurements. The review also discussed the use of smartphones in some wound-sensing systems.

The review shows that pH-responsive wound monitoring can involve different material designs and interpretation methods. A simple colour indicator is one possible approach, while other systems may use more complex optical measurement or digital assistance.

This is relevant to Dermasense because a visual-only system must make its response sufficiently distinguishable and interpretable. If the colour response is ambiguous, a user may not be able to interpret it consistently.

The design should therefore consider the viewing area, colour visibility, lighting conditions, indicator stability, and the need for clear instructions. Any reference colour scale would also need to be developed and tested for the final formulation rather than copied directly from another study.

Reference: *Optically pH-Sensing in Smart Wound Dressings towards Real-Time Monitoring of Wound States* (2025), *Analytica Chimica Acta*, 1350, 343808.

## 7. Key Challenges Identified in Existing Research

Although published studies demonstrate the potential of pH-responsive materials, several challenges remain relevant to the development of a practical wound-monitoring product.

### 7.1 Indicator Leaching

An indicator may move out of the hydrogel when it comes into contact with fluid. This can change the sensing material's performance and raise concerns about exposure.

The studies by Liu et al. (2017) and Hoveizavi et al. (2025) illustrate different approaches to improving indicator retention.

For Dermasense, the selected indicator and hydrogel combination would need to be tested for release under defined conditions.

### 7.2 Reliability of the Colour Response

The visible response may depend on the hydrogel formulation, fluid composition, exposure time, lighting, and other conditions.

A colour transition observed in a simple laboratory solution may not behave identically in a more complex wound-fluid environment.

Dermasense would therefore require controlled testing to establish its response range, repeatability, and sensitivity to interference.

### 7.3 Material Performance

The sensing layer must maintain suitable physical properties while allowing fluid to reach the indicator. Changes in swelling, water content, or mechanical strength could affect the patch's performance.

The material would need to be evaluated as a complete formulation rather than by assuming that the properties of its individual ingredients will remain unchanged after combination.

### 7.4 Clinical Interpretation

A change in pH is not a definitive diagnosis of infection. Wound assessment may require examination of the wound, patient symptoms, medical history, and other clinical information.

A pH-responsive patch should not encourage users to diagnose an infection or delay professional assessment based on colour alone.

### 7.5 Safety and Translation into Practical Use

Laboratory performance does not automatically demonstrate suitability for clinical use. The final formulation would require evaluation of material compatibility, indicator release, stability, manufacturing consistency, and other safety factors relevant to the intended application.

Any clinical or commercial development would also need to address the applicable regulatory requirements.

## 9. Research Gap and Relevance to Dermasense

The reviewed literature demonstrates that hydrogel-based wound dressings and pH-responsive colour indicators have been investigated in published research. Studies have shown that an indicator can be incorporated into a polymer-based dressing and produce a visible response under controlled conditions.

However, these findings do not establish that every indicator or hydrogel formulation will provide the same performance. Indicator retention, fluid interference, mechanical properties, repeatability, and safety remain important considerations for individual designs.

The literature also highlights an important limitation: wound pH is only one parameter associated with wound condition, and current evidence does not justify treating a pH reading as an independent diagnosis of infection.

The main research gap for Dermasense is therefore not whether pH-responsive hydrogel materials are possible. Published studies have already demonstrated such approaches. The remaining question is whether a suitable and reproducible combination of materials can be developed for the specific proposed patch while meeting appropriate performance and safety requirements.

Further work on Dermasense should focus on:

- Comparing candidate hydrogel and indicator combinations.
- Establishing the colour response across a defined pH range.
- Evaluating repeatability and interference from other fluid components.
- Assessing indicator leaching and material stability.
- Investigating material compatibility and safety for the intended application.
- Defining clear limitations for interpreting the visual response.

These steps would help determine whether the proposed concept is technically feasible and what further evidence would be needed.

## 10. Conclusion

The reviewed literature provides a scientific foundation for exploring Dermasense as a hydrogel-based wound patch with a pH-responsive colour indicator.

Research on hydrogel dressings supports investigating their potential as wound-dressing materials, while published colourimetric hydrogel studies demonstrate that visible pH responses can be incorporated into specific polymer systems. Reviews of wound pH also show why such readings must be interpreted carefully, since the relationship between pH and healing is complex and the available clinical evidence has limitations.

Dermasense builds on these research directions by proposing a visual sensing function without electronic components. Its feasibility will depend on the selected formulation, reliable colour response, indicator retention, physical performance, and safety evaluation.
