# Dermasense — Testing and Validation Plan

**Project status:** Academic research and product-design concept.  
**Document purpose:** To outline the testing needed to evaluate a proposed pH-responsive hydrogel wound-dressing concept.  
**Current evidence status:** No validated Dermasense prototype, completed test dataset, clinical evidence, or regulatory approval has been established.

## 1. Objective

Dermasense is being explored as a hydrogel dressing concept with a colour-indicator layer intended to respond to changes in local wound-fluid pH. The first objective is to find out whether a candidate material system can produce a readable and repeatable colour response under controlled laboratory conditions while keeping the indicator contained within the intended layer.

A pH response alone cannot confirm or rule out infection. Wound pH can be influenced by healing stage, wound type, fluid composition, topical products, microorganisms, and dressing materials. The project must therefore be described as a **proposed pH-change indicator or visual wound-monitoring aid**, not as an infection diagnostic.

## 2. Current Design Assumptions to Investigate

The following are research candidates, not final selections:

- **Hydrogel matrices:** Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), PVA/gelatin blends, or alginate-based systems.
- **pH indicators:** Bromothymol blue (BTB) as an initial candidate and phenol red as an alternative for comparison.
- **Layer concept:** A wound-contact fluid-handling hydrogel layer, a separate indicator-containing layer, and a transparent protective film. A skin-friendly adhesive border may be evaluated separately if it remains part of the design.
- **Exploratory pH range:** Approximately pH 5 to 9, subject to refinement after the intended use and literature evidence are reviewed. This is a laboratory screening range, not a clinical threshold or a patient-facing colour guide.

The exact polymer formulation, indicator loading, cross-linking method, layer thickness, adhesive, intended wound type, contact duration, and use conditions have not been finalized. Each must be recorded when selected for an experiment.

## 3. Testing Principles

1. **Define the intended use before setting pass/fail criteria.** The required pH interval and performance targets depend on the specific wound type, users, and intended interpretation.
2. **Set acceptance criteria before collecting results.** Do not choose thresholds after seeing the data. Where evidence is not yet sufficient to justify a threshold, label the result exploratory and document the open question.
3. **Use suitable controls and replicates.** Include the blank hydrogel or matrix without indicator, indicator-containing samples, reference buffers, and other controls appropriate to the test. Determine replicate numbers in the written protocol rather than assuming one specimen is sufficient.
4. **Control test conditions.** Record buffer or fluid composition, measured pH, temperature, sample dimensions, lighting, exposure time, and measurement equipment wherever relevant.
5. **Keep a traceable record.** Give each formulation and specimen a unique ID. Record material lots, preparation details, raw results, images, deviations, analysis methods, and failures.
6. **Do not generalize published results to Dermasense.** A reported result for another formulation does not validate this design.

## 4. Proposed Test Sequence

The order below is a proposed development sequence. It is not a claim that any of the tests have already been performed.

### Stage 1 — Material and Formulation Screening

**Question:** Which candidate hydrogel–indicator combinations are practical enough to evaluate further?

**Proposed work:** Compare candidate matrix options and evaluate whether each can be formed consistently, remain intact during handling, and incorporate the indicator in a sufficiently uniform way. Document preparation variables such as polymer concentration, indicator concentration, cross-linking or curing conditions, thickness, drying conditions, and final dimensions.

**Record:** Formulation ID, ingredient and lot details, preparation method, appearance, thickness, flexibility, visible defects, and any processing issues.

**Decision:** Retain only formulations that can be prepared reproducibly enough for controlled testing. Do not select a formulation based on appearance alone.

### Stage 2 — pH Response and Calibration

**Question:** Does the finished material show a distinguishable and repeatable colour response across the exploratory pH range?

**Proposed work:** Expose samples to appropriately prepared reference buffers spanning the selected test range (initially around pH 5–9). Verify buffer pH with a suitable calibrated meter. Include blank-matrix controls and use the same measurement conditions across samples. The response of the indicator after incorporation into a hydrogel may differ from its response in a simple solution.

**Measure or document:**

- Buffer pH before and after exposure, where relevant.
- Observed colour at each tested pH.
- Colour measurements from a colorimeter or standardized image capture, if available.
- Lighting, camera settings or instrument settings, sample thickness, temperature, and exposure duration.
- Differences between specimens and batches.

A standardized image setup can help compare colours, but photographs alone do not establish analytical accuracy. If image-based measurements are used, lighting, background, distance, and camera settings should be controlled. Any numerical colour metric (for example, CIELAB values) and analysis method should be defined in advance.

**Decision:** Determine whether colour differences are sufficiently distinguishable and repeatable for the intended research purpose. No clinically meaningful colour thresholds have been established for Dermasense.

### Stage 3 — Response Time, Reversibility, and Readability

**Question:** How quickly does the colour change occur, and can the change be read consistently?

**Proposed work:** Record the colour response at predefined time points after exposure to reference buffers. If reversibility is relevant to the proposed use, evaluate repeated changes between selected pH conditions and document hysteresis, drift, or incomplete return to the original colour.

Ask observers who are not involved in preparing the samples to read coded samples under standardized lighting, if a readability study is appropriate. Keep the observer method and scoring rules consistent.

**Record:** Time to a predefined response, colour readings over time, observer agreement or disagreement, and any fading or drift. Define the response criterion before testing; do not invent a passing response time after the results are known.

### Stage 4 — Simulated Wound-Fluid Performance and Interference

**Question:** Does the response remain interpretable in a more realistic fluid environment?

**Proposed work:** After the initial buffer tests, use a documented and appropriate simulated wound-fluid system selected with qualified supervision. Examine whether fluid composition and likely interferents alter the colour response, fluid transport, or material integrity. Possible interference categories to review include salts, proteins, coloured fluids, topical products, and other substances expected in the intended use environment.

Use only approved, documented test materials. Do not use patient samples, fresh human or animal fluids, or clinical material without the appropriate approvals, facilities, and protocols.

**Record:** Fluid formulation and source, measured pH, exposure conditions, colour results, swelling, loss of integrity, and any interference observed. Buffer performance must not be assumed to predict performance in wound fluid.

### Stage 5 — Indicator Containment and Leaching

**Question:** Does the indicator remain in the intended layer, and what amount, if any, is released under test conditions?

**Proposed work:** Evaluate indicator release into a suitable extraction medium using a method with adequate sensitivity and appropriate controls. Compare candidate immobilization or containment approaches where justified. Examine whether release changes with exposure time, pH, temperature, and formulation.

**Record:** Extraction conditions, analytical method and detection limit, calibration information, released indicator measurements, control results, and uncertainties. A visual inspection alone cannot establish that leaching is absent or acceptable.

**Decision:** Any safety-related limit must be justified by the intended exposure and qualified toxicological/biological assessment. This document does not define a safe leaching limit.

### Stage 6 — Physical and Fluid-Management Properties

**Question:** Does the proposed dressing structure retain its intended form and manage fluid as required by the eventual use case?

Potential test categories include:

- Swelling and fluid uptake.
- Thickness and layer uniformity.
- Flexibility, tensile behaviour, and handling integrity.
- Layer separation or delamination.
- Adhesive performance and removal behaviour, if an adhesive is part of the design.
- Water-vapour transmission and oxygen transmission, only where relevant to the design requirement or a proposed claim.
- Transparency and legibility of the indicator through the protective layer.

The test method and target values should be chosen with reference to the intended use and suitable comparators. A favourable result in one property does not establish overall dressing suitability.

### Stage 7 — Stability, Packaging, and Process Compatibility

**Question:** Do storage, light exposure, packaging, and any proposed sterilization process alter the colour response or material performance?

**Proposed work:** Develop a stability plan that considers temperature, humidity, light exposure, packaging conditions, and the proposed storage period. Compare samples before and after relevant exposures. If a sterilization method is being considered for a future product, evaluate its compatibility with the complete material system; do not assume a method suitable for one polymer or indicator is suitable for another.

**Record:** Storage and exposure conditions, packaging configuration, colour drift, physical changes, indicator release, and batch variation. Accelerated ageing can support development work but does not automatically replace real-time stability evidence.

### Stage 8 — Reproducibility and Quality Checks

**Question:** Can the chosen formulation and process produce comparable results on different runs?

After a candidate has passed initial screening, repeat the key tests across independently prepared batches. Establish documented quality checks for dimensions, appearance, indicator distribution, pH response, colour consistency, and any other critical attributes. Define acceptance limits only after the intended requirements, measurement uncertainty, and relevant evidence are understood.

### Stage 9 — Biological Evaluation and Later Evidence

Any evaluation of cytotoxicity, irritation, sensitization, extractables and leachables, or other biological risks must be selected and conducted through qualified laboratories using appropriate methods. Test selection depends on the final device, its materials, the nature and duration of contact, and its intended use. These activities are beyond a basic visual demonstration.

Only after an adequate design, performance, safety, and regulatory plan exists should the team consider whether further preclinical or clinical evaluation is appropriate. Any human study would require the relevant ethics and regulatory review before it begins.

## 5. Suggested Results Table

Create one results table per experiment or use a separate CSV file if the dataset becomes large. At minimum, record:

| Field | What to Record |
|---|---|
| Test ID | Unique experiment identifier |
| Date and operator | When the test was conducted and by whom |
| Formulation ID | Matrix, indicator, and formulation version |
| Material lots | Supplier and lot/batch identifiers where available |
| Sample ID | Unique specimen identifier |
| Conditions | pH, fluid, temperature, time, lighting, and other relevant settings |
| Method and equipment | Procedure version, instrument, calibration status, and analysis method |
| Raw observations | Measurements, photos, notes, and deviations |
| Controls and replicates | Control outcomes and number of specimens tested |
| Analysis | Calculations and summary method |
| Outcome | Pass, fail, or exploratory, against criteria defined beforehand |
| Limitations | Uncertainty, interference, missing data, or follow-up needed |

Do not present illustrative values, planned experiments, or example tables as measured results.

## 6. Development Gates

Progress to the next stage only when the previous stage has generated enough documented evidence to justify it.

1. **Gate A — Defined concept:** Intended use, candidate layer design, and key risks are documented.
2. **Gate B — Bench response:** A reproducible pH response has been measured under controlled conditions.
3. **Gate C — Containment and simulated-fluid checks:** Interference and indicator release have been investigated using documented methods.
4. **Gate D — Physical and stability evaluation:** Material properties and process/storage effects are sufficiently characterized for the next development decision.
5. **Gate E — Qualified safety and regulatory review:** Appropriate biological evaluation and regulatory advice identify the evidence required before any consideration of human use.

Passing an early bench test does not establish clinical usefulness, infection-detection accuracy, or safety for wound application.
